Machine guarding is not just a compliance checkbox — presses, robots and automated lines injure people when access points are unguarded or guarded wrong. Here are the fundamentals of getting light curtains and interlocks right.

Fixed guards first, devices second

The hierarchy is simple: if nobody needs regular access to a hazard, bolt a fixed guard over it. Protective devices — light curtains, interlocked doors — are for access points used routinely: loading a press, clearing a jam, feeding a cell. They allow productivity without removing protection.

Light curtains: Type, resolution, distance

Safety light curtains create an invisible sensing field; breaking it stops the machine. Three parameters define the selection:

  • Type (2 or 4): Type 4 offers the highest fault tolerance and is the norm for press brakes, power presses and serious injury hazards. Type 2 suits lower-risk applications. Your machine risk assessment (per ISO 12100 / ISO 13849) decides — when in doubt, Type 4.
  • Resolution: 14 mm detects fingers (mounted close to the point of operation), 30 mm detects hands, 40–90 mm suits body detection at perimeter guarding. Finer resolution allows closer mounting but costs more per protected height.
  • Safety distance: the curtain must sit far enough from the hazard that the machine stops before a hand arrives — calculated from hand speed (K = 2,000 mm/s in the standard formula), the machine's total stopping time and the curtain's resolution. A curtain mounted too close is a false sense of safety.

Options like muting (temporarily suspending the field while a pallet passes) and blanking (ignoring a fixed obstruction) keep production flowing — but must be engineered, not improvised.

Interlocks: when the guard is a door

Where access is through a physical door or gate, safety interlock switches ensure the machine cannot run with the door open. Two families matter:

  • Interlocks without guard locking — stop the machine when the door opens. Fine when the machine stops quickly.
  • Solenoid-locking interlocks — hold the door shut until the hazard (a spinning centrifuge, a coasting saw) has actually stopped. Essential wherever stopping takes time.

Prefer RFID-coded types over plain mechanical tongues — they resist the screwdriver-and-spare-actuator defeat that plagues older switches.

The logic in between: safety relays

E-stops, curtains and interlocks all feed a safety relay (or safety controller) with force-guided, monitored contacts — the certified element that actually removes power. Standard PLCs and ordinary relays are not substitutes: they fail unpredictably and undetected. Match the relay's category/performance level (Cat. 3 / Cat. 4, PL d / PL e) to the risk assessment, and wire dual-channel wherever the standard calls for it.

Five mistakes we see repeatedly

  • Light curtain mounted too close — machine cannot stop in time
  • Gaps beside or under the curtain that a hand can reach around
  • Interlock defeated with a spare actuator kept "for convenience"
  • Safety devices wired through a standard PLC input instead of a safety relay
  • No documented risk assessment behind the chosen Type/category
