Every temperature measurement starts with the same question: RTD or thermocouple? Both work; each is clearly better in its own territory. Here's how we split the decision in practice.

How they work, in one line each

An RTD (Pt100) is a platinum resistor whose resistance rises predictably with temperature — 100 Ω at 0 °C, hence the name. A thermocouple is a junction of two dissimilar metals that generates a small voltage proportional to temperature.

Choose an RTD Pt100 when…

  • Accuracy matters. A Class A Pt100 achieves ±0.15 °C at 0 °C — several times better than a standard thermocouple. Pharma, food, cold chains and quality-critical processes almost always specify RTDs.
  • The temperature stays below roughly 500 °C. Utilities, reactors, dryers, storage — the RTD range covers most process industries.
  • Stability over years matters. RTDs drift far less than thermocouples, meaning fewer calibration surprises during audits.
  • Wiring runs are moderate. Use a 3-wire circuit as standard; 4-wire where the loop feeds a critical measurement.

Choose a thermocouple when…

  • It's hot. Type K comfortably reaches 1,100 °C, Type R/S up to 1,600 °C — furnaces, kilns, incinerators and molten metal are thermocouple territory. RTDs simply can't go there.
  • You need fast response. A fine-tip, grounded thermocouple responds in fractions of a second — useful on fast thermal cycles like plastic moulding.
  • The sensor takes abuse. Thermocouples tolerate vibration and mechanical shock better than the fine platinum element of an RTD.
  • Budget per point is tight and many points are measured — thermocouples cost less, though remember compensating cable adds cost per metre.

The details that catch people out

Compensating cable: a thermocouple must be wired back with matching compensating/extension cable — copper wire in the middle of the run corrupts the reading. RTDs use ordinary copper.

Junction type: grounded junctions respond faster but can pick up electrical noise; ungrounded junctions are electrically isolated and last longer in electrically noisy plants.

Thermowells: in pressurized, corrosive or flowing media, install the sensor in a thermowell so it can be replaced without draining the line. Match the well's material and insertion length to the process, and keep the sensor tip spring-loaded against the well bottom.

Transmitters: for runs beyond ~20–30 m, or into noisy areas, convert the sensor signal to 4–20 mA with a head-mount temperature transmitter rather than running element wire across the plant.

Quick decision table

  • Below 500 °C + accuracy/stability → RTD Pt100
  • Above 500 °C, fast response, heavy vibration → thermocouple (Type K first, R/S for extremes)
  • Either way: correct sheath material, insertion length and thermowell for the process
