Four sensing technologies cover almost every object-detection job on a machine. Choosing between them takes one question: what are you detecting, and from how far?

Inductive — the metal workhorse

Inductive proximity sensors detect metal only, at short range (typically 1–40 mm), with absolute indifference to dust, oil and ambient light. They have no moving parts and switch millions of cycles without wear. If the target is metal and you can mount the sensor close, this is the default answer — cylinder position, gear teeth, machine slides, metal parts on fixtures.

Watch out for: reduced sensing range on aluminium and brass versus steel (unless you choose factor-1 types), and flush vs non-flush mounting — a non-flush sensor buried in metal false-triggers on its own housing.

Capacitive — sees through walls

Capacitive sensors detect almost anything — plastics, wood, liquids, powders — because they sense change in capacitance rather than metal. Their signature trick is detecting product through a non-metallic container wall: liquid level through a plastic tank, granules through a hopper wall.

Watch out for: sensitivity to material build-up and humidity; most models have an adjustment potentiometer for exactly this reason. Keep them out of splashing water where possible.

Photoelectric — distance and versatility

Photoelectric sensors detect objects optically at ranges from centimetres to tens of metres, in three arrangements:

  • Through-beam — transmitter and receiver face each other; longest range and most reliable, but needs wiring on both sides.
  • Retro-reflective — sensor and a reflector; one-sided wiring, good range, use polarized versions for shiny objects.
  • Diffuse — the object itself reflects the beam; simplest install, shortest range, affected by object colour unless you specify background suppression.

Bottles on a conveyor, cartons at a palletizer, web breaks, parts in a feeder — packaging and material handling run on photoelectrics. For very small parts or tight spaces, fiber optic versions put a millimetre-scale sensing head where nothing else fits.

Watch out for: transparent objects (use specialized clear-object models), heavy dust or steam (consider ultrasonic instead), and mutual interference when sensors face each other.

Ultrasonic — when optics fail

Ultrasonic sensors bounce sound off the target, so colour, transparency and shine are irrelevant — glass bottles, clear film and black rubber all reflect sound equally. They also measure distance continuously, making them a favourite for tank level and roll-diameter (loop) control. They tolerate dust and mist far better than optical sensors.

Watch out for: soft, sound-absorbing targets (foam, loose fabric), strong air currents, and a blind zone directly in front of the sensor face.

Rule-of-thumb summary

  • Metal target, short range → inductive
  • Non-metal target or level through a wall → capacitive
  • Anything at a distance → photoelectric
  • Transparent / any-colour target, dusty air, or distance measurement → ultrasonic
