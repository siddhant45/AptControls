No instrument fails loudly when it drifts — the gauge still points, the display still glows, and the process quietly runs a few degrees or a few hundred millibar away from where you think it is. That gap has a cost.

What drift actually costs

  • Quality: a sterilizer running 2 °C low, a reactor 0.3 bar off, an oven profile shifted — batches get rejected, or worse, shipped.
  • Energy: a boiler pressure transmitter reading low makes the boiler work harder than needed, every hour, invisibly.
  • Audits: for pharma, food and ISO 9001 plants, "no valid calibration record" is among the most common audit findings — and among the easiest to prevent.
  • Safety: pressure relief decisions, interlock setpoints and alarm trips all assume the instrument tells the truth.

What calibration is (and isn't)

Calibration compares your instrument against a reference standard of known, traceable accuracy, at several points across its range, and documents the errors found. If errors exceed tolerance, the instrument is adjusted and re-verified. The output is a record: as-found readings, as-left readings, the reference used and its traceability. Without the record, the work may as well not have happened — that record is what an auditor accepts and what lets you trend drift over the years.

How often?

There is no single legal interval — the right frequency comes from criticality and history:

  • Quality-critical and safety-related loops: every 6–12 months, and after any suspicious event (overpressure, impact, repair).
  • General process monitoring: 12–24 months is common practice.
  • Trend and adjust: if three cycles in a row show negligible drift, you can justify stretching the interval; if an instrument repeatedly drifts out, shorten it — or replace the instrument.

Building a schedule that survives audits

  • Number every instrument (tag ID) and keep a master list with location, range and tolerance
  • Assign each a calibration interval and due date; review the list monthly
  • Keep certificates traceable to national standards, filed against the tag ID
  • Label instruments with the calibration date and due date at the point of use
  • Record as-found and as-left values — the as-found data is your drift history

Calibrate, repair or replace?

For workhorse instruments like pressure gauges, calibration economics matter: a gauge that fails calibration twice is telling you something. Modern transmitters drift far less than mechanical gauges — upgrading chronic offenders often costs less than their calibration history.
